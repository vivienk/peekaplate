# Fix our inputs before blaming TRELLIS.2

Most of what made the 41-dish menu "too bad to share" is our own doing, and most of it is cheap to fix, but a hard core of failures belongs to single-photo reconstruction itself. The ranking, from my reading of the evidence, is: (1) the share page showed the weakest artifact, a **35k-face, 1024-texture mobile mesh rendered unlit with `MeshBasicMaterial`**, which strips every shading cue; (2) the inputs are mostly **512 px JPEG thumbnails** with baked shadows and mixed camera angles; (3) the cutouts keep drop shadows, crop plates, and fuse plate and food, which coincides with ground slabs on three dishes; (4) no mesh clean-up was applied before display; and only then (5) TRELLIS.2's inherent limits, which decide *which* dishes fail: piled, layered, garnish-heavy and glossy food. The model limits are real (a self-reported census put TRELLIS.2 at **91.1% non-watertight** meshes) but they mostly hurt the dishes that are hard for any single-photo method. Better inputs and a lit, cleaned, higher-detail viewer should lift roughly a dozen dishes; the rest need genuine extra views. For restaurants, multi-photo capture and 2.5D relief are realistic; photogrammetry at studio scale and splatting are not for a 41-item menu, and commercial single-image APIs share the same single-view ceiling. Two caveats apply throughout: no one rated dishes individually, and the lit-versus-unlit and full-versus-mobile gaps were inferred from code and texture statistics, not rendered side by side.

## The viewer and export choices showed the weakest version of the output

The share page swapped every material for an unlit `MeshBasicMaterial` (base colour only), added no lights, and loaded the 35k-face mobile GLB; the page itself says "35k-face mobile model in base colour" (measured from the share template, `scratchpad/trellis-share/template.html`, lines 60 and 88-155, as recorded in the pipeline-evidence notes). That swap discards the metallic-roughness map and normals that the GLBs actually contain. With no shading, the only depth cue left is the original photograph's own lighting baked into the texture, so a plate of ramen reads as a sticker. The likely reason for the swap is that a metallic factor of 1.0 renders dark in three.js without an environment map, so this is a tooling shortcut and not a model flaw. The earlier diagnostic renders also warn that a lit viewer on the *raw* mesh would expose noise: the "Normal" and "Clay" modes of the 300k-face Kaggle meshes were speckled and terraced, and dish 12 showed a large square ground plane. Lighting alone is therefore not a fix; it pays off only together with clean-up.

The mobile export compounds this. The full export has **928k-998k faces and 4096 textures**, while the mobile one has **32k-35k faces and 1024 textures**, produced by re-running `to_glb` with `decimation_target=35000, texture_size=1024, remesh=True` from the same latent, not by decimating the full mesh. Face count drops about 28x and texel count 16x. Yet the 4096 texture is not as detailed as it looks: the mean Laplacian sharpness of the pizza's 4096 base-colour map is 12.3, but jumps to 52.4 when the same texture is downsampled to 1024, which means effective colour detail is already about 1024 or less. This matches the architecture, since colours come from a 1024^3 voxel grid that is baked into an atlas, so a 4096 bake adds no detail ([TRELLIS.2 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14692)). The practical reading is that "max" quality bought almost nothing in colour, while the 35k-face decimation is where the silhouette and fine relief (noodles, grains, fried coating) were lost, and the mobile texture is itself 1.5-3x smoother than a clean downsample of the full one. The right middle tier is therefore the 2048-texture, roughly 300k-face export, shown lit, not the mobile mesh unlit.

## Thumbnail photos, shadows and clipped cutouts cap quality before the model runs

About **35 of 41 input photos have a short side of exactly 512 px** (the 41 inputs range from 415 to 2000 px; only dishes 01, 02, 03, 04, 15 and 16 are larger), and a visual check shows soft, JPEG-compressed images with little texture (measured on `results/*/input_image.png`). TRELLIS.2 does not downsize them further: it caps the long side at 1024, crops a square around the foreground, and conditions DINOv3-L at 512 for the sparse structure and 1024 for shape and texture, so a 512 px photo is only upsampled and carries no extra information ([pipeline code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/main/trellis2/pipelines/trellis2_image_to_3d.py), [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14692)). The problem is source detail, not a resolution mismatch. A 2048 px original would be downscaled, so about 1024 px on the dish's long side is the useful ceiling. No published ablation compares 554 px with 2048 px inputs for TRELLIS.2, so the size of the gain is untested, though a maintainer's one anecdotal tip for weak results is to upscale and restyle toward a render-like look ([issue #45](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/issues/45)).

Camera angle is the second input factor. The set mixes viewpoints: pizza (01) and edamame (28) are near top-down, most bowls are 3/4 views at roughly 30-45 degrees, and the bento (16) is an angled flat-lay. The measured result fits the mechanism. Near-overhead dishes come out flat but well formed, while the three dishes that came out compressed into a single flat shell (22, 28, and nearly 20 and 23, with height-to-width ratios of **0.04-0.11**) are piled foods seen from above or obliquely. TRELLIS.2 trained on renders with a 10-70 degree field of view, and the Hunyuan3D and TripoSG papers train on elevations of about -20 to +30 degrees, so a strict 90-degree top-down view is probably out of distribution ([TRELLIS.2 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14692), [Hunyuan3D-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12202), [TripoSG](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.06608)). That is an inference: no source tested overhead food, and TRELLIS.2's own elevation range is not stated in what I could read.

Cutouts are the third factor and the one most plausibly linked to the worst meshes. TRELLIS.2 skips its own background remover when it receives alpha, multiplies RGB by alpha (so the background becomes black), and crops a tight square around pixels above 80% alpha with no margin ([pipeline code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/main/trellis2/pipelines/trellis2_image_to_3d.py)). Our masks came from Kaggle sweep-A cutouts because CPU BiRefNet ran out of memory and the loader's RMBG-2.0 was stubbed out. They keep the plate in every dish, which is right, but 08 (lobster ramen) and 12 (tempura ramen) keep the soft grey drop shadow as foreground, and cutouts for 02, 04, 15, 17, 18 and 19 touch the frame edge (all measured). **Ground slabs** (flat shells spanning most of the footprint) appear in 08 (24% of faces), 12 (22%) and 26 (52%). Shadow is a contributing cause but not a sufficient one: dishes 10, 11 and 14 have similar soft-alpha fractions and no slab. The TRELLIS v1 tracker reports the same "floor beneath the mesh" symptom, which one user cured by switching to RMBG-2.0, and a maintainer says the model "treats the background as part of the object" ([TRELLIS issue #76](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS/issues/76), [TRELLIS.2 issue #45](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/issues/45)). I found no TRELLIS.2 issue naming the slab, so its root cause stays unconfirmed. Finally, black compositing turns any white or grey fringe into a visible edge ring, which is why an eroded, colour-decontaminated alpha matters (an inference from the code, not an ablation).

## Model limits decide which dishes fail, and they are not tunable

Per-dish structure supports a simple split. Eleven dishes are clean (one or two shells, main shell above 99%, few non-manifold edges): 02, 04, 05, 06, 09, 11, 14, 15, 19, 20 and 23. Nine are bad: 26 (88 shells, 2,777 non-manifold edges), 08, 12, 18, 07, 17, 16, 22 and 28 (measured on the mobile GLBs). Simple, whole-contents bowls do well; piles, layers and garnish-heavy plates do not. Another 13 dishes (29-41) had no TRELLIS.2 mesh at all in the 28-dish run, so any 41-dish share was at best two-thirds populated. This pattern is what one expects from a model that never sees the back or the interior.

The documented limits explain it. TRELLIS.2 compresses a 1024^3 asset to about 9.6K latent tokens with a 16x-downsampling VAE; features smaller than a voxel alias, two close parallel surfaces in one voxel blend their material, and small holes occur because the sparse decoder struggles to close a manifold surface ([paper, Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14692)). The model card calls the model a pre-trained base model that is not aligned to preference and may produce small holes ([model card](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/TRELLIS.2-4B)). For food that means herbs, noodles, rice grains and sesame (thin or sub-voxel), and a sauce layer against a plate rim (parallel close surfaces), are the first things to blur or merge. A user-run census of 2,307 models reported TRELLIS.2-4B at **91.1% non-watertight, 14.9% non-manifold, and 100% over 30k triangles**, though it is self-reported with about 100 samples per model ([issue #181](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/issues/181)). Our measured non-manifold edges (up to 2,777) and stray shells follow food complexity, not export settings.

Three further limits are weaker in evidence but worth stating. Training data is Objaverse-XL, ABO, HSSD and TexVerse, filtered for PBR and aesthetics, and no source documents food content, so photographed glossy, translucent, irregular food is probably out of distribution ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.14692)); I could not find any food evaluation. Lighting is baked in despite randomised training lights: a maintainer concedes "PBR estimation has inherent ambiguity" ([issue #43](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/issues/43)), and there is no de-lighting stage, unlike Hunyuan3D-2 ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12202)). Seeds matter: on a navy suit the mean metallic value was 0.000 for one seed and 0.46 for another at `1024_cascade`, while the 512 pipeline stayed non-metallic ([issue #207](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/issues/207), open and unanswered). We ran one seed with default sampler settings (12 Euler steps; guidance 7.5 for shape, 1.0 for texture, per the [pipeline config](https://huggingface.co/microsoft/TRELLIS.2-4B/raw/main/pipeline.json)), so best-of-N seeds is an untested but cheap lever. I found no evidence that tuning steps or guidance helps.

## Multi-view capture and 2.5D are realistic; splatting and studio photogrammetry are not

Multi-photo input is the only change that adds real information. TRELLIS.2 has no official multi-image mode: v1 shipped a tuning-free `run_multi_image` that its authors say may not be optimal, and a TRELLIS.2 pull request (#104, stochastic and averaged-view sampling) was not confirmed merged ([TRELLIS README](https://github.com/microsoft/TRELLIS), [PR #104 mirror](https://gh.r-mj.com/microsoft/TRELLIS.2/pull/104)). Averaging unregistered views at differing angles could blend geometry, and no quantitative test exists, so a food A/B on three or four photos would be our own experiment. Commercial endpoints accept 1-4 images (Tripo recommends 2-4, Meshy 1-4), which is the quickest way to try it without building anything ([Tripo](https://www.tripo3d.ai/blog/multiple-reference-images-3d-generation), [Meshy](https://docs.meshy.ai/en/webapp/image-to-3d)).

Commercial single-image APIs are worth a small bake-off but will not remove the ceiling. A vendor-authored benchmark without food inputs found Tripo best on geometry, Meshy best on single-view texture, at roughly $0.18-0.25 per call and 99-195 s median latency ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/lang/nl/how-i-stress-tested-3-ai-3d-generators-on-the-same-inputs-what-the-numbers-actually-show)). Their own guidance says multi-object scenes merge into one distorted mesh, which describes plate-plus-garnish ([Meshy docs](https://docs.meshy.ai/en/webapp/image-to-3d)). Our earlier research found Tripo v3.1 already wired privately at about $0.30 per dish but untested for lack of credits, and every output stays `publication_eligible: false` under ADR 0004's rule against invented edible surfaces; commercial APIs are therefore a reference, not a publishing path.

Real photogrammetry works for food but at a labour cost that explains why vendors cap menus. Kabaq (now QReal) reportedly shoots roughly 96-114 photos per dish on a turntable ([YourStory via search summary](https://yourstory.com/2017/07/cant-decide-order-kabaq-makes-easy-putting-virtual-3d-food-table), 2017, low confidence), and a Sketchfab tutorial uses 109-211 photos in a light tent, calling lighting "the most important piece" ([Sketchfab](https://sketchfab.com/blogs/community/using-real-life-objects-virtual-augmented-reality-kabaq)). An Indian POS vendor with a competing product quotes Rs 1,500-4,000 per model and says most restaurants model only the top 10-15 items; a 40-item menu would cost Rs 60,000-160,000 ([Petpooja](https://blog.petpooja.com/glossary/ar-menu/)). Glossy sauces and translucent food leave holes ([Univ. Melbourne FAQ](https://ms-kb.msd.unimelb.edu.au/next-lab/3d-scanning/troubleshooting/faq-3d-scanning)). Splatting handles reflections better: on food video, 3DGS reached PSNR 37.83 against 22.74 for Nerfacto ([MetaFood3D](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.01966v2)). But splats are not light GLBs, mesh extraction reintroduces the glossy-geometry problem, and I found no verified splat-to-mobile-GLB pipeline for food. Single-image splat models add licence and payload blockers: Apple's SHARP weights are non-commercial and write about 63 MB per dish (from our earlier report, which cites [arXiv:2512.10685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10685)). For owners, I would rate a 3-4 photo guided capture as plausible and 100-photo turntable capture as realistic only for a handful of hero dishes.

2.5D is the realistic tier for a whole menu. A depth-displaced relief from the owner's own photo, backed by a procedural plate, keeps real pixels and avoids hallucinated backs. Single-image layered-depth work shows the weakness: warping gaps appear where the view moves past the depth edges, and TechCrunch judged the 2018 Facebook version barely better than a picture floating on butcher paper ([3D Photography with LDI](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.04727.pdf), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2018/06/07/how-facebooks-new-3d-photos-work/)). A few degrees of tilt hides most of that. Our earlier research proposed MoGe-2 (MIT) for the depth and normals, with a plan for 150-400 KB GLBs and a 20-30 degree orbit clamp ([MoGe](https://github.com/microsoft/MoGe), [arXiv:2507.02546](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02546)); those size and quality claims are untested on our cutouts. It cannot support table-top AR or orbiting.

## What would actually improve the next run

Run the cheapest controlled experiment first, changing one factor at a time on about six dishes: three from the clean group (05, 09, 20) and three from the bad group (08, 12, 26). First, show the existing 300k-face or full GLBs lit with an environment map (and keep PBR materials) against today's unlit mobile mesh; this isolates the viewer effect, which I rank first but did not measure. Second, run the existing mesh clean-up (drop full-footprint flat shells, small shells, repair non-manifold edges), which already exists in the repo's postprocess but was not in the share path. Third, redo the cutouts with RMBG-2.0 or BiRefNet, erode and decontaminate the alpha, keep the whole plate, exclude cast shadows, and leave a margin so the frame never clips the plate. Fourth, test best-of-4 seeds, because material and colour flip by seed. Fifth, for inputs, swap in or reshoot photos at 1024 px or more on the dish, at a consistent 30-60 degree elevation, in soft diffuse light without harsh speculars; no source tested food, so treat that as the best-supported default, not a proven recipe. Sixth, test 2-4 real photos through Tripo or Meshy multi-image on the hard dishes, and a MoGe-2 relief on all 41 as the fallback tier.

## Conclusion

The central finding is a reframing: the 41-dish failure is mostly a presentation and input-quality problem layered on top of a model that is merely mediocre at food, not a model that cannot do food. That is why the same TRELLIS.2 meshes include about eleven structurally clean dishes next to nine broken ones. The broken ones are predictable (piles, layers, garnish, slabs from shadowed or fused cutouts), so the menu can be triaged by dish type instead of re-run wholesale.

The decision this implies is a split strategy. Use single-photo TRELLIS.2 only for simple bowls and plates, shown lit and cleaned, and treat everything else as either a candidate for 2-4 guided photos or a 2.5D relief. The largest unknowns are the effect sizes: nobody has measured lit versus unlit, 512 versus 1024 px inputs, or multi-image versus single-image for food, and no per-dish human rating exists to validate the structural ranking. A six-dish A/B of the steps above would settle those at a few dollars of compute.
